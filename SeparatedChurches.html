SEPARATED CHURCHES

A Catholic study of Christian divisions, their historical founders, origins and doctrinal differences

Christianity has experienced serious divisions throughout its history. This collection examines churches and religious movements that are not in full communion with the Catholic Church. It explains when they emerged, the people associated with their beginnings, the historical circumstances, and the teachings that differ from Catholic doctrine.

The word founder must be used carefully. Some communities grew out of long historical movements rather than being created by one person. Others were organised by identifiable leaders. A founder's role is not always the same as the origin of every belief later associated with a denomination.

A necessary Catholic distinction

The Catholic Church distinguishes heresy (the obstinate post-baptismal denial or doubt of a truth that must be believed with divine and Catholic faith), apostasy (total repudiation of the Christian faith), and schism (refusal of submission to the Roman Pontiff or communion with those subject to him). These are technical theological terms, not insults to use against individual people.

The Catechism also teaches that people born and raised in communities that resulted from earlier divisions are not personally guilty merely because of the separation. Catholics are to recognise baptised Christians as brothers and sisters in the Lord. Elements of truth, Scripture, faith, hope, charity and grace can be found outside the visible boundaries of the Catholic Church.

Therefore: this page evaluates teachings and historical separations from a Catholic perspective; it does not judge the conscience, sincerity or eternal destiny of individual believers.

16TH CENTURY · GERMANY · REFORMATION

Lutheran Churches

Reformation tradition

Key reformer: Martin Luther (1483–1546)

Martin Luther, an Augustinian friar and professor of theology, became the central figure in the German Reformation. In 1517 he circulated his Ninety-Five Theses, initially challenging the preaching and sale of indulgences and calling for theological debate. The dispute expanded into a break with Rome and the development of Lutheran church communities.

Read full historical and doctrinal profile

Historical development: Luther's movement spread through German territories with the support of scholars, city councils and princes. The Augsburg Confession (1530), prepared principally by Philipp Melanchthon, became a foundational Lutheran confession. Luther did not single-handedly create every later Protestant church.

Key teachings: Lutheran theology emphasises justification by faith, the authority of Scripture, and the distinction between Law and Gospel. It generally recognises Baptism and the Lord's Supper as sacraments, while differing from Catholic teaching on the nature of justification, ecclesial authority, the Eucharistic sacrifice and several other doctrines.

Catholic assessment: The Reformation rejected or reinterpreted teachings and structures that Catholics understand as belonging to the apostolic faith. The Council of Trent responded to disputed doctrines while also addressing abuses requiring reform. The Catholic Church nevertheless recognises that many Lutheran Christians are baptised Christians and that genuine Christian gifts are present in their communities.

Important note: “Protestantism” is not one single church founded by Luther. It includes many traditions with different histories and doctrines.

Read: Romans 3–5; Galatians 2–3; James 2; John 6; Matthew 16:18–19.

Martin Luther: disputed teachings and the Catholic response

What Luther taught: Luther gave central importance to sola scriptura (Scripture as the sole infallible rule of faith) and sola fide (justification by faith alone). He rejected the Pope's claim to universal authority, disputed several Catholic teachings on the Mass and the sacraments, and ultimately rejected the Catholic Church's judgment in the controversy. He did not, however, reject the Trinity, Christ's divinity or the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist in the same way some later Protestants did.

Why the Catholic Church considers these positions erroneous: Catholics hold that Christ entrusted teaching authority to the apostles and their successors, and that Scripture and apostolic Tradition belong together (2 Thessalonians 2:15; 2 Timothy 2:2). The Church teaches that justification is entirely a gift of grace, received through faith, but that grace also renews the person and makes faith active through love (Galatians 5:6; James 2:24). Good works do not purchase salvation; they are the fruit of grace working in a person. The Catholic Church also teaches seven sacraments instituted by Christ and the sacrificial character of the Eucharist.

Catholic judgment: These are serious doctrinal disagreements with historic Catholic teaching and the Reformation caused a grave wound to Christian unity. But the 1999 Joint Declaration on Justification reached a substantial consensus on basic truths, and the 2016 Catholic–Lutheran statement acknowledged both gifts received through the Reformation and the damage caused by division. It would therefore be inaccurate to say that every Lutheran teaching is simply condemned or that every Lutheran is personally a heretic.

Scriptural examination: Matthew 16:18–19; John 20:21–23; 2 Thessalonians 2:15; James 2:14–26; Galatians 5:6; John 6:51–58.

16TH CENTURY · ENGLAND · ENGLISH REFORMATION

Church of England (Anglican Communion)

Reformation tradition

Historical catalyst: King Henry VIII; later reformers included Thomas Cranmer

The Christian Church in England existed centuries before Henry VIII and was historically part of Western Catholic Christianity. The decisive institutional break with papal authority took place in the 1530s. The 1534 Act of Supremacy declared the English king supreme head of the Church in England after the conflict over Henry's marriage and the Pope's authority.

Read full historical and doctrinal profile

Origins: The Church of England's own historical account traces British Christianity to the early centuries and describes its communion with Rome before the Reformation. Henry VIII initiated the break; he did not create Christianity in England. Thomas Cranmer, Archbishop of Canterbury, played a major role in shaping its later Protestant theology and liturgy under Edward VI.

Development: The religious settlement under Elizabeth I helped establish the distinctive Anglican identity. Anglicanism later developed into a worldwide communion with diverse theological expressions, from evangelical to Anglo-Catholic.

Key differences: The Church of England does not recognise the Pope's universal jurisdiction. Anglican beliefs about Eucharist, ordained ministry, authority and sacraments vary among provinces and traditions. Its historic formularies differ from Catholic doctrine on papal primacy and several other points.

Catholic assessment: Anglicanism is not accurately described as a church founded from nothing by Henry VIII. It is an ancient English Christian institution whose communion with Rome was broken in the Reformation. Catholic teaching recognises the complexity of Anglican history, while maintaining that full ecclesial communion and Catholic understanding of apostolic ministry are not present.

Read: Acts 15; Matthew 16:18–19; John 17:20–23.

Henry VIII and Thomas Cranmer: the break with Rome and later doctrinal changes

What Henry VIII did: Henry asserted royal supremacy over the Church in England and rejected the Pope's jurisdiction. This was first and foremost an institutional and ecclesiastical rupture. Henry himself retained many traditional Catholic doctrines and opposed several Protestant reforms; it is historically misleading to present him as the sole author of all later Anglican theology.

What was later taught: Under Archbishop Thomas Cranmer and the reigns that followed, English church teaching moved further in Protestant directions. The English Reformation challenged papal authority and reshaped teaching and worship concerning the Eucharist, justification, the saints and the sacraments. Anglican traditions later became diverse.

Why the Catholic Church considers the separation wrong: Catholic teaching holds that the Bishop of Rome has a real primatial office in the Church, grounded in the Petrine ministry (Matthew 16:18–19; Luke 22:31–32; John 21:15–17). A monarch's civil authority does not give him the spiritual office or jurisdiction entrusted to the apostles and their successors. The Church also maintains the apostolic sacramental ministry and the Eucharistic faith handed down through the Church.

Catholic judgment: Henry's rejection of papal jurisdiction is properly described as schismatic separation; one should not automatically label every action of his reign or every Anglican doctrine as a formal heresy by Henry personally. Later Anglican doctrinal positions must be assessed individually. The Catholic Church's 1896 judgment in Apostolicae Curae declared Anglican ordinations invalid, a specific judgment about orders, not a declaration that Anglican people are without Christian faith.

Scriptural examination: Matthew 16:18–19; Luke 22:31–32; John 21:15–17; Acts 15:1–29; 1 Timothy 3:15.

18TH–19TH CENTURIES · BRITAIN

Methodist Churches

Later Protestant movement

Key founders: John Wesley (1703–1791) and Charles Wesley (1707–1788)

Methodism began as a renewal movement within the Church of England in the 18th century. John Wesley and his brother Charles organised disciplined groups for prayer, Bible study, preaching and works of mercy. The movement later developed separate Methodist denominations, especially in North America and Britain.

Read full historical and doctrinal profile

Key emphases: Personal conversion, grace, holiness, disciplined Christian living, evangelism and care for the poor. Methodist traditions are diverse and some remain in ecumenical relationships with other churches.

Catholic comparison: Methodists generally do not accept papal primacy or the Catholic understanding of the Church's sacramental and apostolic structure. Their theology of grace and sanctification has points of contact with Catholic teaching, but also significant differences.

Historical caution: Wesley did not begin by setting out to found an entirely new church; Methodism developed from a renewal movement into separate church bodies over time.

John Wesley: teachings and Catholic evaluation

What Wesley taught: Wesley stressed prevenient grace, conversion, assurance of faith, sanctification and Christian perfection. He remained Trinitarian and affirmed the divinity of Christ. Methodism emerged as a renewal movement within Anglicanism and only later became institutionally separate.

Where Catholic teaching differs: Wesley's movement did not accept papal primacy or the Catholic Church's full understanding of apostolic succession and sacramental authority. Some Methodist formulations of assurance, perfection and the relationship between faith and works differ from Catholic formulations. However, Wesley's emphasis that grace comes first and transforms the believer is not in itself contrary to Catholic teaching.

Why a blanket charge of heresy would be inaccurate: Catholic theology distinguishes a serious doctrinal error from the canonical offence of heresy, which requires obstinate denial or doubt of a truth to be believed with divine and Catholic faith. Wesley's Trinitarian Christianity and many of his teachings on grace overlap with historic Christian belief. The separation from Catholic communion and disagreements over authority and sacraments should be explained precisely rather than falsely claiming that every Methodist distinctive is heretical.

Scriptural examination: John 15:1–5; Philippians 2:12–13; 1 Corinthians 12:4–13; Ephesians 2:8–10.

1865 · EAST LONDON · 1878 NAME ADOPTED

The Salvation Army

Evangelical Protestant movement

Co-founders: William Booth (1829–1912) and Catherine Booth (1829–1890)

The Salvation Army began in 1865 as the East London Christian Mission, led by the Methodist preacher William Booth and his wife Catherine. It adopted the name “The Salvation Army” in 1878 and developed a distinctive military-style organisation for evangelism and practical service to people in poverty.

Read full historical and doctrinal profile

Purpose and work: The Booths sought to preach the Gospel among people who were often neglected by established churches. The movement became known for shelters, food assistance, addiction recovery and social outreach. Its charitable work is a significant part of its public identity.

Doctrinal identity: The Salvation Army describes itself as an evangelical part of the universal Christian Church. It emerged from Methodist/Wesleyan theology and emphasises conversion, holiness, the Bible and practical Christian service.

Sacraments: A distinctive feature is that the Salvation Army does not practise water baptism or the Lord's Supper as rites in the usual way, believing that holiness and spiritual life should not be limited to sacramental observances. This differs substantially from Catholic sacramental theology, in which Baptism and the Eucharist are central sacraments instituted by Christ.

Catholic assessment: Its origins are Protestant and outside Catholic communion. Its service to the poor and proclamation of Christ should be distinguished from doctrinal questions; Catholics can acknowledge genuine works of charity while explaining the importance of the sacraments and apostolic communion.

William and Catherine Booth: sacramental disagreement

What the Booths' movement taught: The Salvation Army developed an evangelical and holiness tradition that does not administer water Baptism or the Lord's Supper as church rites. It emphasises inward conversion, holiness and service, and regards Christian life as not dependent on sacramental ceremonies.

Why the Catholic Church considers this teaching erroneous: Catholics believe Christ explicitly commanded Baptism (Matthew 28:19; John 3:5) and instituted the Eucharist as his memorial and sacramental presence (Luke 22:19–20; 1 Corinthians 11:23–29). The sacraments are not merely human ceremonies or optional symbols; they are visible signs instituted by Christ through which grace is given. The Council of Trent reaffirmed the seven sacraments and their importance in the life of the Church.

Catholic judgment: Rejecting the sacramental practice of Baptism and Eucharist conflicts with Catholic doctrine. Nevertheless, it is important to distinguish this doctrinal criticism from the Salvation Army's extensive charitable service and from judgments about the personal culpability of William or Catherine Booth or present-day Salvationists. The Salvation Army itself is a later evangelical movement, not an ancient Church founded by Christ or the apostles.

Scriptural examination: Matthew 28:19; John 3:5; Luke 22:19–20; 1 Corinthians 10:16–17; 1 Corinthians 11:23–29.

1830 · NEW YORK · RESTORATIONIST

Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (LDS)

Restorationist movement

Founder: Joseph Smith Jr. (1805–1844)

Organised on 6 April 1830 in New York, the movement teaches that the original Church established by Jesus Christ had lost its authority and needed to be restored through Joseph Smith. Members commonly prefer the name “Latter-day Saints”; “Mormon” is a familiar historical label, but the official church name is preferred.

Read full historical and doctrinal profile

Foundational claims: Joseph Smith reported a First Vision in 1820 and later said that the angel Moroni directed him to records he translated as the Book of Mormon. The movement accepts the Bible and additional scriptures, including the Book of Mormon, Doctrine and Covenants, and Pearl of Great Price.

Major doctrinal differences: LDS teaching understands the Father, Son and Holy Ghost as three distinct beings united in purpose, rather than the one divine Being in three co-equal, consubstantial Persons confessed in the Nicene Creed. It also teaches continuing revelation through living prophets and a restoration of priesthood authority. These are fundamental differences from historic Catholic and Nicene Christianity.

Catholic assessment: The Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith issued a formal negative decision in 2001 on the validity of baptism administered by the LDS Church, explaining that its understanding of God and the Trinity differs so substantially that the Catholic Church cannot recognise that baptism as valid. This is a specific sacramental judgment, not a judgment on the sincerity or dignity of individual LDS members.

Important distinction: LDS members identify themselves as Christians and centre their faith on Jesus Christ. Catholic theology, however, finds their conception of God and other foundational teachings incompatible with the classical Trinitarian faith.

Joseph Smith: restoration claims and non-Nicene teachings

What Smith's movement taught: Joseph Smith claimed that the original Church and its authority had been lost and restored through him. LDS scripture and later teaching include additional books beyond the Bible, continuing revelation through living prophets, and a conception of the Godhead in which the Father, Son and Holy Ghost are distinct beings united in purpose. Historic LDS teachings also include the exaltation of human beings and a view of God that differs fundamentally from classical Christian theology.

Why the Catholic Church considers these teachings fundamentally erroneous: The Christian faith confessed at Nicaea and Constantinople teaches one eternal God in three distinct Persons—Father, Son and Holy Spirit—of one divine substance, not three gods united by agreement. God is eternal and uncreated; he is not an exalted human being who became divine. The Church also teaches that the public revelation entrusted to the apostles is complete in Christ; later private revelations cannot replace or correct that deposit of faith. See Deuteronomy 6:4; Isaiah 43:10; John 1:1–3; Matthew 28:19; 2 Timothy 3:16–17; Jude 3.

Formal Catholic ruling: In 2001 the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith answered negatively when asked whether LDS baptism is valid. Its explanation states that the LDS understanding of Father, Son and Holy Spirit is so different from the Christian doctrine of the Trinity that the same words do not express the same Trinitarian faith. The Vatican reaffirmed this reasoning in Gestis Verbisque (2024). This is a precise sacramental and doctrinal judgment; the 2001 explanation explicitly says it is not a judgment on the dignity or sincerity of LDS members.

Catholic judgment: These teachings are not simply a variation within Nicene Christianity; they contradict foundational Christian doctrines about the one God and the Trinity. This is the clearest example on this page of a movement whose foundational theology departs from the historic Christian creed.

19TH CENTURY · UNITED STATES

Seventh-day Adventist Church

Later Protestant movement

Key figures: Ellen G. White, James White, Joseph Bates; roots in the Millerite movement

The Seventh-day Adventist Church was formally organised in 1863 after the Millerite movement and the disappointment surrounding expectations of Christ's return in 1844. It is not the work of one sole founder; several leaders shaped its early identity.

Read full historical and doctrinal profile

Distinctive emphases: Saturday Sabbath observance, the imminent Second Coming, health and lifestyle teaching, and a distinctive interpretation of the heavenly sanctuary. Ellen G. White became an influential author and spiritual leader.

Catholic comparison: Adventists share many central Christian beliefs, including the Trinity and the divinity of Christ, but differ from Catholic teaching on the authority of tradition, the Sabbath and Sunday, the Church, and certain eschatological interpretations.

Early Adventist leaders: Sabbath and distinctive prophetic interpretations

What the founders and early leaders taught: The Adventist movement arose from the Millerite expectation of Christ's return. Early leaders including Ellen G. White, James White and Joseph Bates promoted Saturday Sabbath observance and distinctive interpretations of the events of 1844 and Christ's ministry in the heavenly sanctuary. Adventists generally affirm the Trinity and the divinity of Christ, unlike non-Trinitarian restorationist groups.

Why Catholics disagree: Catholics hold that the first day of the week became the Christian day of Eucharistic worship because Christ rose on Sunday and the apostolic Church gathered on that day (Acts 20:7; 1 Corinthians 16:2; Revelation 1:10). Catholics do not accept the Adventist claim that Saturday observance is binding on all Christians as a test of faith, nor do they accept the distinctive 1844 sanctuary interpretation as apostolic doctrine. The New Covenant fulfils the ceremonial provisions of the Mosaic law in Christ (Colossians 2:16–17).

Catholic judgment: These are important disagreements, but Adventists affirm many central doctrines of historic Christianity, including the Trinity. It is therefore inaccurate to call the entire denomination non-Christian or to describe every Adventist belief as heresy. The claims should be examined individually against Scripture and apostolic Tradition.

Scriptural examination: Acts 20:7; 1 Corinthians 16:2; Colossians 2:16–17; Revelation 1:10; Hebrews 4:9–10.

LATE 19TH CENTURY · UNITED STATES · BIBLE STUDENT MOVEMENT

Jehovah’s Witnesses

Non-Trinitarian restorationist movement

Key founders and organisers: Charles Taze Russell (early Bible Student movement); Joseph Franklin Rutherford (organisation adopted the name “Jehovah’s Witnesses” in 1931)

The movement grew out of Bible study groups associated with Charles Taze Russell in the 1870s in the United States. The Watch Tower organisation developed under later leadership, especially Joseph Franklin Rutherford. The name “Jehovah’s Witnesses” was adopted in 1931, so it is historically inaccurate to attribute every later teaching directly to Russell alone.

Read full historical and doctrinal profile

Central beliefs: Jehovah’s Witnesses reject the doctrine of the Trinity; they teach that Jesus is God's Son but is not Almighty God, identify the pre-human Jesus with Michael the archangel, and understand the Holy Spirit as God's active force rather than a divine Person. They teach that Christ began ruling invisibly in 1914, that 144,000 will reign with Christ in heaven, and that most faithful people hope for everlasting life on a restored earth. Their worship and organisational practices also differ from historic Catholic Christianity.

Blood policy: Jehovah’s Witnesses refuse transfusions of whole blood. As of September 2026, their official announcement says acceptance of red cells, white cells, plasma or platelets is a matter of individual conscience, while the prohibition on whole-blood transfusions remains. This policy should be described accurately and not repeated using outdated blanket claims about every blood component.

Catholic assessment: The central disagreement is not merely the use of God's name or a different style of worship. Their denial of the Trinity and of Christ's full, eternal divinity contradicts the Nicene faith professed by the Catholic Church and the historic creeds of mainstream Christianity. Their understanding of the Holy Spirit also differs from the Christian doctrine of the Trinity.

Charles Taze Russell and Joseph F. Rutherford: disputed teachings and the Catholic response

What the movement taught: Russell's Bible Student movement rejected the Trinity and developed a non-Trinitarian reading of Scripture. Under Rutherford, the organisation became more distinct and adopted the name Jehovah’s Witnesses in 1931. The movement teaches that Jesus is a created heavenly being, identified as Michael the archangel before and after his earthly life, rather than the eternal, consubstantial Son of God. It regards the Holy Spirit as God's impersonal active force, not a divine Person. It also teaches a distinctive chronology in which Christ began ruling invisibly in 1914.

Why the Catholic Church considers these teachings heretical in content: The Catholic faith confesses one God in three Persons—Father, Son and Holy Spirit—each fully divine and eternal. The Son is not a creature: “the Word was God” and all things came to be through him (John 1:1–3); the Son is above the angels and is addressed in divine terms (Hebrews 1:5–12). The Holy Spirit is not an impersonal force: Scripture speaks of the Spirit as one who teaches, wills and can be grieved (John 14:26; 1 Corinthians 12:11; Ephesians 4:30), and Peter identifies lying to the Holy Spirit with lying to God (Acts 5:3–4). The Church therefore rejects the movement’s denial of the Trinity and the full divinity of Christ as incompatible with the apostolic faith.

About the 144,000 and 1914: Catholics read Revelation's 144,000 within its apocalyptic and symbolic context, not as proof that only a fixed small group can share heavenly life with Christ (Revelation 7:4–9). The claim that Christ began an invisible royal presence in 1914 is a distinctive interpretation of biblical chronology, not a doctrine taught by the apostles or the historic creeds. Catholics instead confess that Christ will come again in glory, while the exact day and hour are not known (Matthew 24:36; Acts 1:7; 1 Thessalonians 4:16–17).

Blood and Christian moral teaching: Jehovah’s Witnesses interpret biblical commands to abstain from blood as prohibiting whole-blood transfusions. Catholics agree that blood is sacred and that Scripture forbids consuming blood, but do not regard a medically necessary transfusion as eating blood or as intrinsically forbidden. Moral decisions in healthcare should consider the preservation of life, informed consent and the full clinical circumstances; this page is not individual medical advice.

Catholic judgment: The movement's non-Trinitarian doctrines contradict foundational Christian dogma. In traditional theological language, denial of the Trinity and of the Son's full divinity is heretical in content. That does not mean every individual Witness is personally guilty of the canonical offence of heresy: personal culpability requires knowledge and obstinacy and cannot be presumed. Catholics should explain the doctrine clearly while treating each person with charity and respect.

Scriptural examination: Deuteronomy 6:4; John 1:1–3; John 14:16–17, 26; John 20:28; Acts 5:3–4; 1 Corinthians 8:6; Hebrews 1:5–12; Matthew 24:36.

What does the Catholic Church teach about Christian division?

1. The Church is one. Catholic doctrine teaches that Christ founded one Church and prayed that his disciples would be united (John 17:20–23). The divisions of Christians wound that visible unity.

2. Division has a history. The causes of separation are not identical. The East–West schism, the 16th-century Reformation, later revival movements and 19th-century restorationist movements arose in different circumstances. They should not all be described as if they began in the same way.

3. Recognise what is true and holy. The Second Vatican Council teaches that separated Christian communities retain elements of Christian truth and sanctification. Catholics should approach other Christians with respect, honesty and a desire for unity.

4. Work for unity in truth. Catholic ecumenism is not pretending that doctrinal differences do not matter. It involves prayer, conversion of heart, accurate understanding, dialogue and a search for the unity Christ desires.

Heresy, schism and separation — not the same thing

Heresy: In Catholic canon law, the obstinate denial or doubt, after Baptism, of a truth that must be believed with divine and Catholic faith.

Schism: The refusal of submission to the Roman Pontiff or of communion with the members of the Church subject to him.

Apostasy: The total repudiation of the Christian faith.

A community may be separated from full communion without every member being personally a heretic or schismatic. These terms describe specific theological and canonical realities. Use them carefully and explain the actual doctrine in dispute.
