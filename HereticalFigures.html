CONSTANTINE · FAITH · HISTORY · DOCTRINE

HERETICAL FIGURES

A Catholic historical study of disputed teachers, doctrinal errors and movements that challenged the apostolic faith

Across Christian history, influential teachers and religious founders have advanced ideas that the Catholic Church judged incompatible with essential doctrines of the faith. This page examines their teachings, historical settings and influence. It begins with ancient controversies such as Arianism and also considers modern founders whose claims depart from historic Christian teaching.

The purpose is theological and historical examination—not personal abuse. The word heresy has a precise meaning in Catholic canon law; it must not be used casually as a synonym for “someone I disagree with.”

How this page uses the word “heretical”

Canon 751 defines heresy as the obstinate denial or obstinate doubt, after Baptism, of a truth that must be believed with divine and Catholic faith. A teaching can be objectively contrary to Catholic dogma (“heretical in content”) without this page claiming that a particular individual incurred the canonical offence. Personal culpability depends on factors that cannot simply be presumed.

For founders of movements outside Catholic communion, the page describes their documented claims and the Catholic assessment of those claims. It does not pretend that every such person was formally judged by a Catholic tribunal or that every follower knowingly shares every teaching.

4TH CENTURY · ALEXANDRIA · ARIAN CONTROVERSY

Arius

Arianism · Christological controversy

Central dispute: Whether the Son is eternal and fully divine, consubstantial with the Father, or a created being subordinate to Him.

Historical and doctrinal examination

Arius was a presbyter in Alexandria whose teaching provoked a major controversy in the early fourth century. He taught that the Son did not share the Father's eternal existence in the same way and that “there was when he was not.” The controversy was not a minor disagreement: it concerned the identity of Jesus Christ and the Christian understanding of salvation.

Catholic judgment: The Council of Nicaea in 325 rejected the Arian position and confessed the Son as “begotten, not made, consubstantial with the Father.” The Council of Constantinople in 381 reaffirmed the Nicene faith. Catholic doctrine confesses Jesus Christ as true God and true man, eternally begotten, not created.

Scriptural study: John 1:1–3; John 8:58; John 20:28; Colossians 1:15–17; Hebrews 1:1–12.

2ND CENTURY · SINOPE / ROME

Marcion of Sinope

Marcionism · Radical separation

Central dispute: Marcion rejected the Old Testament and distinguished the God of Israel from the Father proclaimed by Jesus.

Historical and doctrinal examination

Marcion was a second-century teacher who promoted a sharp division between the Creator described in Israel's Scriptures and the God revealed by Jesus. He rejected the Old Testament and circulated a restricted collection of Christian writings, centred on an edited Gospel of Luke and selected Pauline letters.

Catholic judgment: The Church rejected this division. The God of Israel is the Father of Jesus Christ; the Old and New Testaments belong to one unfolding history of salvation. The Church receives the apostolic Scriptures rather than a privately edited canon.

Scriptural study: Luke 24:27; Matthew 5:17; 2 Timothy 3:16; Romans 11:17–29.

4TH–5TH CENTURIES · BRITAIN / ROME / NORTH AFRICA

Pelagius

Pelagian controversy · Grace and sin

Central dispute: The necessity of divine grace for salvation and the effects of original sin.

Historical and doctrinal examination

Pelagius was an ascetic teacher whose ideas were debated in the Latin Church in the early fifth century. The controversy concerned whether human beings could attain righteousness through their natural powers and moral effort without the indispensable interior assistance of God's grace.

Catholic judgment: The Church rejected Pelagian propositions that denied the necessity of grace. Catholic teaching holds that grace precedes, accompanies and enables every saving response; human freedom is real, but it cannot save itself apart from God.

Scriptural study: John 15:5; Romans 5:12–21; Ephesians 2:8–10; Philippians 2:12–13.

5TH CENTURY · CONSTANTINOPLE · CHRISTOLOGICAL CONTROVERSY

Nestorius

Nestorian controversy · Christology

Central dispute: How to express the unity of Christ's divine and human natures and the title Theotokos for Mary.

Historical and doctrinal examination

Nestorius became Bishop of Constantinople in 428. His preaching and terminology concerning Mary as Theotokos (“God-bearer”) generated a dispute with Cyril of Alexandria. The controversy culminated in the Council of Ephesus in 431, which affirmed the unity of Christ's person and upheld the title Theotokos.

Historical caution: Modern historians debate how accurately the label “Nestorian” represents Nestorius's own mature Christology and the later Church of the East. This profile therefore explains the controversy and the council's doctrinal decision rather than reducing a complex history to a slogan.

Catholic judgment: Jesus Christ is one divine Person in two natures, truly God and truly man. Mary is rightly called Mother of God because the one she bore according to the flesh is the eternal Son of God incarnate.

Scriptural study: John 1:14; Luke 1:43; Galatians 4:4; Colossians 2:9.

3RD CENTURY · ROME / NORTH AFRICA · TRINITARIAN CONTROVERSY

Sabellius

Sabellianism · Modalism

Central dispute: Whether Father, Son and Holy Spirit are truly distinct divine Persons.

Historical and doctrinal examination

Sabellius is associated with a form of modalistic teaching, often called Sabellianism, which explained Father, Son and Holy Spirit as modes or manifestations of the one God rather than three really distinct Persons.

Catholic judgment: The Church confesses one God in three distinct, co-eternal Persons. The Father is not the Son, the Son is not the Spirit, and yet each is fully God; this is not belief in three gods.

Scriptural study: Matthew 3:16–17; Matthew 28:19; John 14:16–17; 2 Corinthians 13:13.

19TH CENTURY · UNITED STATES · LDS MOVEMENT

Joseph Smith Jr.

Latter-day Saint movement · Restoration claims

Central claims: A restoration of the original Church through new revelations and additional scriptures.

Historical and doctrinal examination

Joseph Smith organised the Church of Christ in 1830 in New York. He reported visions and presented the Book of Mormon as an additional witness of Jesus Christ. The movement later developed into the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.

Catholic assessment: LDS teaching about the Godhead differs fundamentally from the Nicene doctrine of one God in three consubstantial Persons. The Catholic Church's doctrinal office ruled in 2001 that baptisms administered by the LDS Church are not valid, because its understanding of Father, Son and Holy Spirit differs substantially from the Church's Trinitarian faith. This is a specific sacramental judgment, not a judgment on the dignity or sincerity of individual members.

Scriptural study: Deuteronomy 6:4; John 1:1–3; Matthew 28:19; Jude 3.

LATE 19TH CENTURY · UNITED STATES · BIBLE STUDENT MOVEMENT

Charles Taze Russell

Early Bible Student movement

Central claims: Non-Trinitarian interpretations of God and Christ that later became associated with Jehovah's Witnesses.

Historical and doctrinal examination

Russell was the central early organiser of Bible Student groups in the 1870s. The modern organisation of Jehovah's Witnesses developed under later leadership, especially Joseph F. Rutherford, and adopted its present name in 1931. It is inaccurate to attribute every later Witness teaching directly to Russell.

Catholic assessment: Jehovah's Witnesses reject the Trinity and the full eternal divinity of the Son, identifying the pre-human Jesus with Michael the archangel. These positions contradict the Nicene confession that the Son is true God, eternally begotten and not created. The doctrinal criticism concerns the teaching; it does not establish the personal culpability of every adherent.

Scriptural study: John 1:1–3; John 20:28; Hebrews 1:5–12; Acts 5:3–4.

20TH CENTURY · SPAIN · PALMARIAN MOVEMENT

Clemente Domínguez y Gómez

Palmarian movement · Rival papal claim

Central claim: A private visionary's assertion that he had been chosen to replace the legitimate Pope.

Historical and doctrinal examination

Domínguez became the central leader of the Palmarian movement, which grew around alleged apparitions at El Palmar de Troya. After Pope Paul VI died in 1978, Domínguez claimed the papacy as Gregory XVII and established a separate hierarchy.

Catholic assessment: The Catholic Church does not recognise this rival papal claim or the Palmarian hierarchy as the continuation of the Catholic Church. The claim that private revelations can appoint a replacement pope and transfer the centre of the Church away from the successor of Peter is profoundly incompatible with Catholic ecclesiology. The alleged apparitions were not approved as authentic by the competent Catholic authority.

Scriptural study: Matthew 16:18–19; Luke 22:31–32; John 21:15–17; 1 John 4:1.

20TH CENTURY · KOREA · UNIFICATION MOVEMENT

Sun Myung Moon

Unification movement · Messianic claims

Central claims: Moon presented himself as the one appointed to complete a mission he believed Jesus had not completed during his earthly life.

Historical and doctrinal examination

Sun Myung Moon founded the Unification movement in South Korea in the twentieth century. Its teachings, especially as expressed in the Divine Principle, reinterpret the mission of Jesus, the meaning of the Cross, marriage and the history of salvation. Moon and his movement made claims about his own providential or messianic role.

Catholic assessment: Catholic Christianity confesses that Jesus Christ's saving work is definitive and that no later religious leader completes a supposedly failed mission of Christ. The Unification movement's account of Jesus and salvation therefore conflicts with central Christian teaching. This page describes the movement's published claims; it does not claim that Moon was subject to a formal Catholic canonical trial for heresy.

Scriptural study: John 19:30; Hebrews 7:24–27; Hebrews 9:24–28; Jude 3.

19TH CENTURY · ENGLAND · SALVATION ARMY

William Booth

Salvation Army · Methodist-derived movement

Historical role: William Booth (1829–1912), together with his wife Catherine Booth, founded the Christian Mission in East London in 1865. The organisation adopted the name The Salvation Army in 1878.

Historical and Catholic doctrinal examination

His life and work: Booth was formed in the Wesleyan Methodist tradition and became an evangelist concerned with reaching people neglected by established churches. His preaching and social mission developed into the Christian Mission. Catherine Booth was a major partner in the movement's ministry, public teaching and advocacy for women's preaching. The Salvation Army became known for evangelism alongside practical relief, including shelters, soup kitchens, support for people experiencing poverty and campaigns for social reform. William Booth died in 1912.

What the Salvation Army teaches: Its official eleven doctrines affirm one God in three co-equal Persons, the full divinity and humanity of Jesus Christ, the inspiration of Scripture, Christ's atonement, repentance, grace, resurrection and final judgment. Therefore, it would be inaccurate to describe the Salvation Army as Arian or as rejecting the Trinity.

Key difference from Catholic sacramental practice: The Salvation Army traditionally does not administer water baptism or Holy Communion as church rites. It teaches that these outward ceremonies are not necessary conditions for salvation and emphasises inward spiritual realities. Catholic teaching, by contrast, recognises Baptism and the Eucharist as sacraments instituted by Christ and essential to the Church's sacramental life; Baptism is necessary for salvation in the way taught by Christ, while the Eucharist is the source and summit of Christian life. This is a substantial ecclesiological and sacramental disagreement.

Catholic assessment: The Salvation Army is a Protestant Christian movement with a broadly Trinitarian and evangelical confession, not a movement whose founder should simply be placed alongside Arius or other ancient teachers condemned for denying central dogmas. Its rejection of sacramental rites and its distinct church structure differ from Catholic doctrine and practice. This page does not claim that William Booth was formally condemned by the Catholic Church as a heretic; it evaluates particular theological differences with precision.

Scriptural study: Matthew 28:19; John 3:5; John 6:51–58; Acts 2:38–42; 1 Corinthians 10:16–17; 1 Corinthians 11:23–29; Ephesians 4:4–6.

Test every teaching

Christians are called to test teachings rather than follow impressive personalities blindly. A teacher's charisma, claimed visions, predictions, miracles, or large following do not by themselves prove that a message comes from God.

Read: Matthew 7:15–20; Acts 17:11; Galatians 1:8; 1 John 4:1; 2 Peter 2:1–3.

The Catholic approach is to examine a claim in the light of Sacred Scripture, apostolic Tradition and the Church's authentic teaching office. A doctrinal error should be named clearly, while every human person is treated with dignity and charity.
