HERETICAL FIGURES

A Catholic study of influential doctrinal errors, historical controversies and modern false teachings

This collection examines influential teachers and movements whose doctrines were judged incompatible with essential Christian teaching by the Catholic Church or the early ecumenical councils. It explores ancient controversies and modern claims that distort the Gospel, the identity of Christ, the Trinity, revelation or the authority of the Church.

The purpose is historical and theological education: to explain what was taught, why it mattered, how the Church responded, and how Christians can discern teaching carefully.

A Catholic and historically careful approach

Heresy is a precise theological and canonical term, not a general insult. A formal canonical offence involves the obstinate denial or doubt, after Baptism, of a truth that must be believed with divine and Catholic faith. A teaching may be described as heretical in content without presuming the personal guilt of every person associated with it.

Historical labels require care. “Arianism,” for example, became an umbrella term for several related fourth-century positions, not a perfectly uniform party. This page distinguishes historical evidence, scholarly interpretation and Catholic doctrinal judgment.

Ancient heretical teachers and controversies

4TH CENTURY · ALEXANDRIA · CHRISTOLOGY

Arius and Arianism

Denied the Son's consubstantial divinity

Principal figure: Arius (c. 256–336), a presbyter in Alexandria. He taught that the Son was not eternal in the same way as the Father and was not of the same divine substance, placing the Son among created beings rather than confessing Him as eternally God.

Historical and Catholic assessment

The Council of Nicaea (325) confessed the Son as consubstantial with the Father: begotten, not made, and fully divine. Constantinople (381) reaffirmed the Church's Trinitarian confession. The controversy included varied positions; not every bishop later labelled “Arian” held exactly the same formula.

Scripture: John 1:1–3; John 20:28; Colossians 1:15–17; Hebrews 1:1–12.

2ND CENTURY · ROME · BIBLICAL CANON

Marcion of Sinope

Rejected the Old Testament

Principal figure: Marcion (2nd century). He sharply separated the God of the Old Testament from the Father proclaimed by Jesus, rejected the Old Testament as Christian Scripture and promoted a reduced collection of Christian writings.

Historical and Catholic assessment

The Church defended the unity of the two Testaments: the Creator and the Father revealed by Jesus are the same God. Catholic interpretation reads the Old and New Testaments together, with Christ as their fulfilment.

Scripture: Matthew 5:17; Luke 24:27; John 1:1–3; 2 Timothy 3:16.

5TH CENTURY · BRITAIN / ROME · GRACE

Pelagius and Pelagianism

Error concerning grace and original sin

Principal figure: Pelagius (late 4th–early 5th century), a British ascetic teacher. Pelagian teaching was associated with an overemphasis on unaided human ability to obey God and rejection of important aspects of the Church's teaching on original sin.

Historical and Catholic assessment

Augustine of Hippo and African councils responded to the controversy. Catholic teaching insists that grace comes first: no one can merit the initial grace of conversion. Human freedom is real, but it is healed and enabled by grace.

Scripture: John 15:5; Romans 5:12–21; Ephesians 2:8–10; Philippians 2:12–13.

5TH CENTURY · CONSTANTINOPLE · CHRISTOLOGY

Nestorius and the Nestorian Controversy

Dispute over the unity of Christ

Principal figure: Nestorius, Patriarch of Constantinople (428–431). The controversy concerned the unity of Christ's divine and human natures and the title Theotokos (“God-bearer”) for Mary.

Historical and Catholic assessment

The Council of Ephesus (431) affirmed the unity of Christ's person and upheld Theotokos. Modern scholarship cautions that “Nestorian” can oversimplify the historical dispute and the later Church of the East; explain the doctrine rather than casually labelling all its descendants.

Scripture: John 1:14; Luke 1:43; Galatians 4:4; Colossians 2:9.

3RD CENTURY · TRINITARIAN THEOLOGY

Sabellius and Modalism

Denied personal distinctions

Principal figure: Sabellius, associated with a third-century form of Monarchianism. Modalism is commonly described as treating Father, Son and Holy Spirit not as distinct divine Persons, but as modes or manifestations of one divine Person.

Historical and Catholic assessment

The Catholic faith confesses one God in three distinct Persons, not three gods and not one Person merely appearing in three roles. The Father is not the Son; the Son is not the Holy Spirit; each is fully and eternally God.

Scripture: Matthew 3:16–17; Matthew 28:19; John 14:16–17; 2 Corinthians 13:13.

4TH CENTURY · LAODICEA · INCARNATION

Apollinaris of Laodicea

Incomplete account of Christ's humanity

Principal figure: Apollinaris, bishop of Laodicea. Apollinarianism taught that in Christ the divine Logos took the place of the rational human mind or soul, compromising the completeness of His humanity.

Historical and Catholic assessment

The Son assumed a complete human nature: body, rational soul, intellect and will, united in the one divine Person. The Council of Constantinople (381) rejected Apollinarianism.

Scripture: Luke 2:52; John 1:14; Hebrews 2:14–18.

Modern false teachings and claims to examine

Not every modern error has one founder or belongs to one denomination. These examples describe recurring teachings and patterns, rather than accusing people by rumour or making unsupported claims about named living individuals.

MODERN · NON-TRINITARIAN CHRISTOLOGY

Modern Arian-like teachings

Denial of Christ's eternal divinity

Some groups describe Jesus as a created being, an exalted angel or a lesser divine figure rather than the eternal, consubstantial Son of the Father.

Catholic response

These positions repeat a central Arian error in substance, even when they do not use that name. The Son is true God from true God, begotten not made. See John 1:1–3; John 8:58; John 20:28; Hebrews 1:5–12.

MODERN · CLAIMS OF NEW REVELATION

Self-appointed messiahs and prophets

Claims that override the Gospel

Individuals may claim to be a new incarnation of Christ, the exclusive final prophet or the only person authorised to reveal a new saving message. Such claims require scrutiny, especially when demanding unquestioning loyalty.

Catholic response

Public Revelation is complete in Christ and the apostolic age; private revelations do not complete or correct Christ's definitive Revelation. See Hebrews 1:1–2; Galatians 1:8; Catechism §§ 66–67.

MODERN · PROSPERITY GOSPEL

Prosperity and transactional religion

Distorts the Gospel of the Cross

Some preachers promise that donations, positive declarations or sufficient faith guarantee wealth, health or success, and may blame suffering people for hardship.

Catholic response

The Gospel does not promise every faithful person wealth or freedom from illness. Christ calls disciples to take up the cross, serve the poor and trust God's providence. See Mark 8:34–36; Luke 12:15; 1 Timothy 6:5–10.

MODERN · ECCLESIAL AUTHORITY

Groups claiming an exclusive replacement Church

Rival authority and schism

Some movements claim the historic Church has disappeared or become apostate and that their own leader or small organisation alone has restored the true Church.

Catholic response

Examine such claims against Christ's promises, apostolic succession, Church history and evidence for the claimant's authority. A private vision or self-declaration does not establish a new apostolic office. See Matthew 16:18–19; Matthew 28:20; John 21:15–17.

MODERN · FABRICATED RELIGIOUS CLAIMS

Conspiracy-based religious teachers

Fear and fabricated evidence

Some online personalities attach invented prophecies, fabricated quotations or conspiracy theories to Christian teaching, presenting speculation as certain doctrine and discouraging source-checking.

Catholic response

Test claims, check original documents, distinguish doctrine from opinion and reject spiritual manipulation. A confident tone, large following or claimed miracle is not proof of truth. See Acts 17:11; 1 Thessalonians 5:21; 1 John 4:1.

MODERN · SPIRITUAL ABUSE

Leaders who claim immunity from accountability

Abuse of spiritual authority

Warning signs include claiming that questioning a leader means opposing God, demanding secrecy, exploiting followers or threatening divine punishment for leaving.

Catholic response

Christian authority is service, not domination. Abuse must not be concealed under obedience or forgiveness. Protect victims, preserve evidence and contact appropriate civil and ecclesiastical authorities. See Mark 10:42–45; 1 Peter 5:2–3.

How to assess a modern teacher fairly

Identify the exact statement in full context; verify it from the person's own published material; compare it with Scripture and the Catechism; check for formal Church judgments; distinguish doctrinal error from disputed opinion; and never infer someone's inner guilt or eternal destiny. Do not publish allegations about named people without reliable, attributable evidence and a clear public-interest reason.
